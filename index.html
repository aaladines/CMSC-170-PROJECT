Problem Statement and Motivation

Motivation

Many agricultural products cannot be stored for a long time such as vegetables and root crops due to their short shelf life. They spoil quickly and cannot be stored for a long time, which means that a drop in supply may lead to higher prices.

If the prediction of the production of vegetable crop in a province is expected to be lower than the same quarter of the previous year, the government, in connection with agricultural traders from other provinces that have higher production can supply it to the province that needs to more just to stabilize prices. In contrast, if the production were to increase, farmers and traders could supply it to other provinces that are in need.

Problem Statement

Can KNN, Gaussian Naive Bayes, Perceptron predict whether a province's quarterly production of a vegetable or root crop increases or decreases compared with the same quarter of the previous year using past data?

Dataset Description

Overview and Size

The dataset includes a variety of vegetable and root crop quarterly, semestral, and annual production data for every province in the Philippines from 2010-2026. After preprocessing, the dataset has 9920 rows.

Source

Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA)

Features and Target

To accurately forecast whether a province's quarterly yield for top vegetable and root crops (based on Philippine agricultural crop production data) will increase (Class 1) or decrease/remain stagnant (Class 0) compared to the exact same quarter of the previous calendar year.

Preprocessing Steps

  • The dataset was prepared by keeping the ten selected crops and regional production records, excluding the Negros Island Region (NIR), provincial records, and the national total.
  • Semester and annual totals were removed, leaving only quarterly observations grouped by crop and region.
  • Region names were cleaned by removing leading dots and extra spaces, and production values were converted into numeric form.
  • Missing values marked as ".." were skipped, while actual zero-production values were preserved.
  • Each quarter was paired with the same quarter of the previous year, and pairs with missing observations were excluded.
  • Production was labeled 1 if it increased and 0 if it decreased.

Data Splitting (Training/Testing)

In training and splitting the data, the group used 80% for training and 20% for testing, applied separately within each year instead of once over the whole dataset. The rows of each year were assigned to the training or testing set using a random number generator, so that the testing data is not biased toward the later years or toward any one quarter, such as Q4. For the dataset, the total amount of available data was 9920 rows, hence the amount of available training and testing rows are 7936 and 1984, respectively. This gives 512 training and 128 testing rows for each year from 2011 to 2025, and 256 training and 64 testing rows for 2026.

Methods

K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)

KNN is an instance-based learning algorithm that classifies a new data point by finding the k most similar historical samples in the dataset and taking a majority vote of their outcomes. This method is highly appropriate for our problem because agricultural patterns are often cyclical and localized. Provinces with similar recent production trends and seasonal positions are likely to experience similar future outcomes. Because KNN makes no underlying assumptions about the mathematical distribution of the data, it can effectively capture these localized, repeating patterns that a global model might overlook. By calculating the Euclidean distance between standardized features (such as previous year-over-year changes and log volumes), KNN will predict whether a province's quarterly production will increase or decrease based on what occurred during historically similar quarters.

Gaussian Naive Bayes

Gaussian Naive Bayes is a probabilistic, generative classifier based on Bayes' Theorem that estimates the mean and variance of each feature for both the "increase" and "decrease" classes. It is suitable for this problem because our dataset consists of continuous numerical features (such as production volume in metric tons), allowing the Gaussian variant to model these features without requiring us to discretize or categorize the continuous data. While the assumption of feature independence is likely violated in time-series agricultural data (since lagged production changes are correlated), using Gaussian Naive Bayes serves as an excellent probabilistic baseline that contrasts sharply with the other chosen methods.

Perceptron

The Perceptron is a linear classifier that iteratively learns a weight vector and bias to draw a separating hyperplane between the two classes. This algorithm is appropriate for our project because it directly tests whether agricultural production increases and decreases can be linearly separated within our feature space. It provides a simple, efficient linear baseline that heavily contrasts with both the instance-based approach of KNN and the probabilistic approach of Naive Bayes. By passing normalized input features through a step activation function and updating weights over several epochs, the Perceptron will attempt to learn a definitive boundary. If the agricultural data proves too noisy or not linearly separable, observing its failure to converge will provide valuable insights for our evaluation discussion.

Evaluation Metrics

To evaluate the performance of the three implemented machine learning algorithms, namely, Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and Perceptron, we used four evaluation metrics: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-score.

Variables

  • True Positive (TP): The actual label is 1 and the predicted label is also 1.
  • True Negative (TN): The actual label is 0 and the predicted label is also 0.
  • False Positive (FP): The label is 0, but the predicted label is 1.
  • False Negative (FN): The label is 1, but the predicted label is 0.

Accuracy measures the proportion of correctly predicted records out of all testing records. It provides an overall measure of how often the model makes correct predictions.
Accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN)

Precision measures the proportion of records predicted as an increase that actually increases. It indicates how reliable the model's positive predictions are.
Precision = TP / (TP + FP)

Recall measures the proportion of actual increases that are correctly identified by the model. It indicates how well the model detects increases in crop production.
Recall = TP / (TP + FN)

F1 score provides a combined measure of both Precision and Recall. This is useful for assessing the balance between identifying actual increases and avoiding incorrect increase predictions.
F1 score = (2 * Precision * Recall) / (Precision + Recall)

Discussion

Overall Performance and the ~60% Baseline

Across the 16-year evaluation period (2011–2026), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) (k = 19, p = 2) emerged as the strongest overall classifier among the three models, recording the highest 16-year average Accuracy (61.96%), Precision (64.17%), and F1-Score (63.86%), and achieving its peak accuracy of 72.66% in both 2012 and 2024. Meanwhile, Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB) led in 16-year average Recall (69.41%), and Perceptron ranked second in average F1-Score (61.30%).

Despite these relative differences, all three models hovered near the ~60% mark (58.01%–61.96% average Accuracy and 61.10%–63.86% average F1-Score). Because the models rely exclusively on three historical lag features—previous year's same-quarter production, prior year-over-year change, and prior quarter-over-quarter change—they capture baseline seasonal momentum during stable years but hit a predictive ceiling whenever external climatic or economic shocks disrupt normal agricultural cycles.

Perceptron Unreliability and Non-Linear Separability

Among the three algorithms, the Perceptron proved unreliable for this dataset. Across all 16 yearly splits, the Perceptron consistently failed to converge (reaching max_epochs = 100 in every year), and adjusting either the learning rate (η) or the maximum number of epochs did not resolve this behavior.

By the Perceptron Convergence Theorem, a single-layer Perceptron converges only when the classes are strictly linearly separable. Because ten distinct vegetable and root crops across sixteen Philippine regions are compressed into a three-dimensional historical lag space—without meteorological or soil variables, and with missing lag records defaulting to 0.0—Class 1 (increase) and Class 0 (decrease or stagnation) instances overlap heavily. Starting from zero weights, changing the learning rate merely rescales the weight vector without altering the flat separating hyperplane, causing the decision boundary to oscillate across noisy records rather than stabilize.

External Factors Driving Yearly Fluctuations (2011–2026)

The sharp spikes, dips, and plateaus in the yearly line graphs align closely with documented climatic, biological, and economic events in Philippine agriculture:

  • 2011–2014 (Post-La Niña Recovery and Stable Growth): Following recovery from the 2010–2011 La Niña episodes, Philippine vegetable and root crop production experienced steady, predictable year-over-year expansion across major producing regions (PSA, 2015). Because historical lag patterns remained consistent, all three models peaked in 2012—with GNB and Perceptron reaching 75.00% accuracy and GNB achieving 100.00% recall and an 85.71% F1-score—while sustaining strong F1-scores (70.06%–76.57%) through 2014.
  • 2015–2016 (Severe El Niño Drought): A strong El Niño event beginning in 2015 and peaking in early 2016 caused prolonged dry spells, reduced irrigation water, and lower yields across Luzon, Visayas, and Mindanao (DOST-PAGASA, 2016; FAO, 2016). Because pre-drought lag features still pointed upward, GNB's recall collapsed from 93.59% (2014) to 39.44% (2015) and 20.00% (2016). KNN buffered this shift better in 2015 (72.73% F1-score) through local neighbor matching, whereas Perceptron adjusted its hyperplane to retain 80.00% recall in 2016 despite dropping to 50.00% accuracy.
  • 2017–2019 (Post-Drought Rebound, Typhoon Ompong, and Input Cost Pressures): Normalizing rainfall in 2017 restored steady production growth, lifting GNB's F1-score back to 80.75%. Performance declined again in 2018–2019 (with F1-scores dropping to 27.16%–55.07% in 2019) due to severe crop damage from Typhoon Mangkhut (Ompong) in late 2018, localized dry spells in 2019, and higher fuel and farm input inflation that squeezed small-scale vegetable output (PSA, 2019; World Bank, 2019).
  • 2020–2022 (COVID-19 Supply-Chain Disruptions and Reopening): In 2020, community quarantine lockdowns disrupted inter-provincial transport, market logistics, and farm labor availability, creating erratic regional surpluses and deficits (World Bank, 2020; FAO, 2021). Perceptron captured all positive recovery cases (100.00% recall; 72.00% F1-score), while KNN and GNB rebounded in 2021–2022 (74.00%–76.92% F1-score) as domestic trade and food service demand normalized.
  • 2023–2024 (2023–2024 El Niño, Late-Year Typhoons, and High-Precision / Low-Recall Skew): In 2023, the transition into El Niño and erratic monsoon rainfall caused non-cyclical regional yield swings that contradicted prior-year trends, dragging all models to their lowest accuracies (46.09%–53.12%) and precisions (38.71%–45.45%) (DOST-PAGASA, 2024; PSA, 2024). By 2024, intensifying El Niño drought conditions followed by successive late-year typhoons (including Kristine, Leon, and Pepito) triggered widespread production contractions (DA, 2024; PSA, 2025). With Class 0 dominating the year, models predicted decreases conservatively—raising overall accuracy (69.53%–72.66%) and giving KNN an 85.71% precision on the few increases it flagged, while collapsing recall to 15.00% (GNB and KNN) and 27.50% (Perceptron) because localized rebounds could not be detected without weather indicators.
  • 2025–2026 (La Niña Transition and Partial-Year Coverage): Post-El Niño recovery and La Niña rainfall patterns in 2025 restored model recall (66.15%–81.54%), while 2026 covers only Q1 and Q2 (64 test records instead of 128), producing moderate F1-scores (32.65%–59.46%) from half-year seasonal data (PSA, 2026).

Limitations and Recommendations

The primary limitation of this study is its reliance on only three historical production features, omitting exogenous drivers such as rainfall volume, temperature anomalies, El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) indices, typhoon paths, soil conditions, and economic indicators like fertilizer and fuel prices. Additionally, assigning 0.0 when prior-year lag records are unavailable introduces artificial flat trends into the feature space.

Because KNN, Gaussian Naive Bayes, and Perceptron all plateaued around ~60%—and because linear boundaries cannot separate noisy agricultural time-series data—future work should explore non-linear, ensemble, and sequence-aware methods better suited to this dataset.

Conclusion

Among the three machine learning algorithms implemented from scratch for quarterly Philippine agricultural yield prediction (2011–2026; 9,920 records), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) (k = 19, p = 2) proved to be the most effective and consistent classifier. Across 16 deterministic 80/20 per-year splits, KNN achieved the highest 16-year average Accuracy (61.96%), Precision (64.17%), and F1-Score (63.86%)—ranking first or tied in Accuracy in 10 out of 16 years and in Precision in 9 out of 16 years, while maintaining a strong average Recall of 68.79%.

KNN outperformed Gaussian Naive Bayes (61.10% average F1-Score) and Perceptron (61.30% average F1-Score) because its non-parametric, distance-based approach matches test quarters against historically similar regional production trajectories without requiring strict feature independence or linear separability. Tuning the neighborhood size to k = 19—centered within the theoretical √Ntrain window—smoothed out short-term quarterly noise while avoiding the severe non-convergence instability observed in the Perceptron.

At the same time, KNN's ~62%–64% performance ceiling highlights that historical production lags alone cannot fully anticipate external shocks such as the 2015–2016 and 2023–2024 El Niño droughts, typhoons, or pandemic supply disruptions. Building on KNN's strong local-similarity foundation by incorporating meteorological variables (rainfall, temperature, and ENSO indices), crop/region encodings, and non-linear ensemble or time-series models offers a clear path toward higher forecasting accuracy in future work.
