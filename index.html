Problem Statement and Motivation

Motivation

Many agricultural products cannot be stored for a long time such as vegetables and root crops due to their short shelf life. They spoil quickly and cannot be stored for a long time, which means that a drop in supply may lead to higher prices.

If the prediction of the production of vegetable crop in a province is expected to be lower than the same quarter of the previous year, the government, in connection with agricultural traders from other provinces that have higher production can supply it to the province that needs to more just to stabilize prices. In contrast, if the production were to increase, farmers and traders could supply it to other provinces that are in need.

Problem Statement

Can K-Nearest Neighbor, Gaussian Naive Bayes, Perceptron predict whether a province's quarterly production of a vegetable or root crop increases or decreases compared with the same quarter of the previous year using past data?

Dataset Description

Overview and Size

The dataset includes a variety of vegetable and root crop quarterly, semestral, and annual production data for every region and province in the Philippines from 2010–2025 (with partial-year 2026 records excluded so every evaluation year has complete four-quarter coverage). After preprocessing, the dataset has 9,600 rows spanning 2011–2025.

Source

Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA)

Features and Target

  • Raw Dataset Fields: Crop name (10 selected vegetable and root crops), Philippine region (16 regions), year (2010–2025), quarter (Q1–Q4), and quarterly production volume (in metric tons).
  • Engineered Input Features (3): (1) Log previous-year same-quarter production volume, (2) prior year-over-year log production change, and (3) prior quarter-over-quarter log production change.
  • Target Variable: Binary classification indicating whether a region's quarterly crop yield increased (Class 1) or decreased/remained stagnant (Class 0) compared to the exact same quarter of the previous calendar year.

Preprocessing Steps

  • The dataset was prepared by keeping the ten selected crops and regional production records, excluding the Negros Island Region (NIR), provincial records, the national total, and incomplete 2026 columns.
  • Semester and annual totals were removed, leaving only quarterly observations grouped by crop and region.
  • Region names were cleaned by removing leading dots and extra spaces, and production values were converted into numeric form.
  • Missing values marked as ".." were skipped, while actual zero-production values were preserved.
  • Each quarter was paired with the same quarter of the previous year, and pairs with missing observations were excluded.
  • Production was labeled 1 if it increased and 0 if it decreased.

Data Splitting (Training/Testing)

In training and splitting the data, the group used 80% for training and 20% for testing, applied separately within each year instead of once over the whole dataset. The rows of each year were assigned to the training or testing set using a random number generator, so that the testing data is not biased toward the later years or toward any one quarter, such as Q4. For the dataset, the total amount of available data was 9,600 rows, hence the amount of available training and testing rows are 7,680 and 1,920, respectively. This gives a uniform split of 512 training and 128 testing rows for every year from 2011 to 2025.

Methods

Each year from 2011 to 2025 is evaluated independently using the same deterministic 80/20 split (512 training and 128 testing records per year). Across all three classifiers, every regional crop quarter is represented by three continuous logarithmic features engineered from dataset_prepared.csv: (1) log previous-year same-quarter volume, log(1 + prev_q); (2) prior year-over-year log change from the preceding year's matching quarter; and (3) prior quarter-over-quarter log change from the immediately preceding quarter (defaulting to 0.0 when earlier historical quarters are unavailable).

Why We Chose These Methods

K-Nearest Neighbors, Gaussian Naive Bayes, and Perceptron were selected because each algorithm uses a different approach to classification. This allows the study to compare how probabilistic, distance-based, and linear classification methods perform when predicting changes in vegetable and root crop production.

K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) was selected because it classifies records based on the labels of their nearest neighbors. This method helps determine whether crop-region records with similar historical production patterns also have similar production outcomes.

Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB) was chosen because it uses probability to classify records based on their features. It provides a relatively simple and efficient approach for examining how historical production patterns relate to increases or decreases in production.

Perceptron was included to evaluate a linear classification approach. It learns by adjusting its weights and bias based on classification errors, which allows the study to examine whether a linear decision boundary can distinguish between production increases and decreases.

Comparing these three algorithms provides a way to examine how different classification approaches handle patterns in the historical agricultural dataset and identify their respective strengths and limitations.

K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)

In our implementation (k_nearest_neighbor.py), KNN classifies each test crop-region quarter by comparing its recent production scale and growth trajectory against historical records within the same evaluation year:

  • Per-Year Z-Score Standardization: Before computing distances, each year's training features are standardized using their training mean and standard deviation ((x - mean) / std, setting std = 1.0 if zero variance occurs), and the same parameters are applied to that year's test set so large log-volume values do not overpower smaller log-change features.
  • Minkowski / Euclidean Distance (p = 2): For every standardized test record, the model calculates the Euclidean distance d(x, y) = (Σ|xi - yi|2)1/2 across all three features against every training sample in that year.
  • Neighborhood Voting (k = 19): Guided by the square-root heuristic (k ≈ √Ntrain = √512 ≈ 22.63) as an upper guide and empirical validation of odd candidates nearby, the classifier selects k = 19—an odd count chosen to prevent binary voting ties, avoid over-smoothing across distinct crop-region records, and maximize 15-year average Accuracy (0.6245) and F1-Score (0.6426)—assigning Class 1 (production increase) or Class 0 (decrease/stagnant) by majority vote.

Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB)

In our implementation (gaussian_naive_bayes.py), Gaussian Naive Bayes models the continuous distribution of our three logarithmic production features directly without discretization:

  • Class Separation and Empirical Priors: Within each yearly training split, records are grouped into Class 0 (decrease) and Class 1 (increase) to compute prior probabilities P(c) = nc / Ntrain, along with each class's feature-wise sample mean (μc,j) and variance (σ2c,j) on the raw logarithmic features.
  • Variance Smoothing (k = 1e-9): To prevent division-by-zero or numerical underflow when a crop subset has minimal variation, a smoothing term ε = 10-9 × max(σ2) is added to every class-feature variance.
  • Gaussian Likelihood Classification: For each test quarter, the model evaluates the univariate Gaussian probability density P(xj | c) across all three features and multiplies them by the class prior, predicting Class 1 whenever P(x | 1)·P(1) ≥ P(x | 0)·P(0) and Class 0 otherwise.

Perceptron

In our implementation (perceptron.py), the Perceptron tests whether quarterly production increases and decreases can be separated by a single linear hyperplane in three-dimensional feature space:

  • Standardization and Bipolar Label Mapping: Each yearly split undergoes Z-score standardization fitted on the training set, while binary target labels 0 and 1 are mapped to -1 (decrease) and +1 (increase).
  • Iterative Weight Updates (η = 0.01, Max Epochs = 100): Starting from zero weights (w = [0, 0, 0]) and zero bias (b = 0.0), the model passes through the yearly training records for up to 100 epochs, computing net = w·x + b and applying a signum step activation (+1 if net ≥ 0, else -1).
  • Error-Driven Boundary Adjustment: Whenever a training record is misclassified (error = y - prediction ≠ 0), weights and bias update via w ← w + 0.01 × error × x and b ← b + 0.01 × error, stopping early only if an entire epoch produces zero updates.

Evaluation Metrics

To evaluate the performance of the three implemented machine learning algorithms, namely, Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and Perceptron, we used four evaluation metrics: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-score.

Variables

  • True Positive (TP): The actual label is 1 and the predicted label is also 1.
  • True Negative (TN): The actual label is 0 and the predicted label is also 0.
  • False Positive (FP): The label is 0, but the predicted label is 1.
  • False Negative (FN): The label is 1, but the predicted label is 0.

Accuracy measures the proportion of correctly predicted records out of all testing records. It provides an overall measure of how often the model makes correct predictions.
Accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN)

Precision measures the proportion of records predicted as an increase that actually increases. It indicates how reliable the model's positive predictions are.
Precision = TP / (TP + FP)

Recall measures the proportion of actual increases that are correctly identified by the model. It indicates how well the model detects increases in crop production.
Recall = TP / (TP + FN)

F1 score provides a combined measure of both Precision and Recall. This is useful for assessing the balance between identifying actual increases and avoiding incorrect increase predictions.
F1 score = (2 * Precision * Recall) / (Precision + Recall)

Findings

K-Nearest Neighbors emerged as the strongest overall algorithm under the 80/20 per-year split, achieving the highest 15-year average Accuracy (0.6245), Precision (0.6464), and F1-Score (0.6426), while ranking first or tied in accuracy in 9 out of 15 years and precision in 8 out of 15 years.

Gaussian Naive Bayes ranked second overall in Accuracy (0.6026), Precision (0.6111), and F1-Score (0.6300), while recording the highest 15-year average Recall (0.7247)—ranking first or tied in recall in 10 out of 15 years and F1-Score in 8 out of 15 years.

Perceptron recorded the lowest 15-year averages across all four metrics, with 0.5833 average Accuracy, 0.6030 average Precision, 0.6754 average Recall, and 0.6142 average F1-Score.

Discussion

Overall Performance and the ~60% Baseline

Across the 15-year evaluation period (2011–2025), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) (k = 19, p = 2) emerged as the strongest overall classifier among the three models, recording the highest 15-year average Accuracy (62.45%), Precision (64.64%), and F1-Score (64.26%), and achieving its peak accuracy of 72.66% in both 2012 and 2024. Meanwhile, Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB) ranked second in Accuracy (60.26%), Precision (61.11%), and F1-Score (63.00%) while leading in average Recall (72.47%), and Perceptron placed third across all four metrics (58.33% Accuracy; 61.42% F1-Score).

Despite these relative differences, all three models hovered near the ~60% mark (58.33%–62.45% average Accuracy and 61.42%–64.26% average F1-Score). Because the models rely exclusively on three historical lag features—previous year's same-quarter production, prior year-over-year change, and prior quarter-over-quarter change—they capture baseline seasonal momentum during stable years but hit a predictive ceiling whenever external climatic or economic shocks disrupt normal agricultural cycles.

Perceptron Unreliability and Non-Linear Separability

Among the three algorithms, the Perceptron proved unreliable for this dataset. Across all 15 yearly splits, the Perceptron consistently failed to converge (reaching max_epochs = 100 in every year), and adjusting either the learning rate (η) or the maximum number of epochs did not resolve this behavior.

By the Perceptron Convergence Theorem, a single-layer Perceptron converges only when the classes are strictly linearly separable. Because ten distinct vegetable and root crops across sixteen Philippine regions are compressed into a three-dimensional historical lag space—without meteorological or soil variables, and with missing lag records defaulting to 0.0—Class 1 (increase) and Class 0 (decrease or stagnation) instances overlap heavily. Starting from zero weights, changing the learning rate merely rescales the weight vector without altering the flat separating hyperplane, causing the decision boundary to oscillate across noisy records rather than stabilize.

External Factors Driving Yearly Fluctuations (2011–2025)

The sharp spikes, dips, and plateaus in the yearly line graphs align closely with documented climatic, biological, and economic events in Philippine agriculture:

  • 2011–2014 (Post-La Niña Recovery and Stable Growth): Following recovery from the 2010–2011 La Niña episodes, Philippine vegetable and root crop production experienced steady, predictable year-over-year expansion across major producing regions (PSA, 2015). Because historical lag patterns remained consistent, all three models peaked in 2012—with GNB and Perceptron reaching 75.00% accuracy and GNB achieving 100.00% recall and an 85.71% F1-score—while sustaining strong F1-scores (70.06%–76.57%) through 2014.
  • 2015–2016 (Severe El Niño Drought): A strong El Niño event beginning in 2015 and peaking in early 2016 caused prolonged dry spells, reduced irrigation water, and lower yields across Luzon, Visayas, and Mindanao (DOST-PAGASA, 2016; FAO, 2016). Because pre-drought lag features still pointed upward, GNB's recall collapsed from 93.59% (2014) to 39.44% (2015) and 20.00% (2016). KNN buffered this shift better in 2015 (72.73% F1-score) through local neighbor matching, whereas Perceptron adjusted its hyperplane to retain 80.00% recall in 2016 despite dropping to 50.00% accuracy.
  • 2017–2019 (Post-Drought Rebound, Typhoon Ompong, and Input Cost Pressures): Normalizing rainfall in 2017 restored steady production growth, lifting GNB's F1-score back to 80.75%. Performance declined again in 2018–2019 (with F1-scores dropping to 27.16%–55.07% in 2019) due to severe crop damage from Typhoon Mangkhut (Ompong) in late 2018, localized dry spells in 2019, and higher fuel and farm input inflation that squeezed small-scale vegetable output (PSA, 2019; World Bank, 2019).
  • 2020–2022 (COVID-19 Supply-Chain Disruptions and Reopening): In 2020, community quarantine lockdowns disrupted inter-provincial transport, market logistics, and farm labor availability, creating erratic regional surpluses and deficits (World Bank, 2020; FAO, 2021). Perceptron captured all positive recovery cases (100.00% recall; 72.00% F1-score), while KNN and GNB rebounded in 2021–2022 (74.00%–76.92% F1-score) as domestic trade and food service demand normalized.
  • 2023–2025 (2023–2024 El Niño, Late-Year Typhoons, and La Niña Recovery): In 2023, the transition into El Niño and erratic monsoon rainfall caused non-cyclical regional yield swings that contradicted prior-year trends, dragging all models to their lowest accuracies (46.09%–53.12%) and precisions (38.71%–45.45%) (DOST-PAGASA, 2024; PSA, 2024). By 2024, intensifying El Niño drought conditions followed by successive late-year typhoons (including Kristine, Leon, and Pepito) triggered widespread production contractions (DA, 2024; PSA, 2025). With Class 0 dominating the year, models predicted decreases conservatively—raising overall accuracy (69.53%–72.66%) and giving KNN an 85.71% precision on the few increases it flagged, while collapsing recall to 15.00% (GNB and KNN) and 27.50% (Perceptron) because localized rebounds could not be detected without weather indicators. Post-El Niño recovery and La Niña rainfall patterns in 2025 restored model recall (66.15%–81.54%) and lifted GNB and Perceptron F1-scores back to ~65.00% (PSA, 2026).

Limitations and Recommendations

The primary limitation of this study is its reliance on only three historical production features, omitting exogenous drivers such as rainfall volume, temperature anomalies, El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) indices, typhoon paths, soil conditions, and economic indicators like fertilizer and fuel prices. Additionally, assigning 0.0 when prior-year lag records are unavailable introduces artificial flat trends into the feature space.

Because KNN, Gaussian Naive Bayes, and Perceptron all plateaued around ~60%–64%—and because linear boundaries cannot separate noisy agricultural time-series data—future work should explore non-linear, ensemble, and sequence-aware methods better suited to this dataset.

Conclusion

Among the three machine learning algorithms implemented from scratch for quarterly Philippine agricultural yield prediction (2011–2025; 9,600 records), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) (k = 19, p = 2) proved to be the most effective and consistent classifier. Across 15 deterministic 80/20 per-year splits (512 training and 128 testing records per year), KNN achieved the highest 15-year average Accuracy (62.45%), Precision (64.64%), and F1-Score (64.26%)—ranking first or tied in Accuracy in 9 out of 15 years and in Precision in 8 out of 15 years, while maintaining a strong average Recall of 69.45%.

KNN outperformed Gaussian Naive Bayes (63.00% average F1-Score) and Perceptron (61.42% average F1-Score) because its non-parametric, distance-based approach matches test quarters against historically similar regional production trajectories without requiring strict feature independence or linear separability. Tuning the neighborhood size to k = 19—guided by the √512 ≈ 22.63 heuristic and empirical validation across all 15 years—smoothed out short-term quarterly noise while avoiding the severe non-convergence instability observed in the Perceptron.

At the same time, KNN's ~62%–65% performance ceiling highlights that historical production lags alone cannot fully anticipate external shocks such as the 2015–2016 and 2023–2024 El Niño droughts, typhoons, or pandemic supply disruptions. Building on KNN's strong local-similarity foundation by incorporating meteorological variables (rainfall, temperature, and ENSO indices), crop/region encodings, and non-linear ensemble or time-series models offers a clear path toward higher forecasting accuracy in future work.
